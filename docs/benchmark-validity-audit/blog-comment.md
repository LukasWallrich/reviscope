# Checking the answer key for AI peer review

Peer review consumes an enormous amount of researchers' time, yet substantial errors survive it. [Aczel et al. (2021)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34776003/) estimated that researchers spent more than 100 million hours reviewing papers in 2020. In psychology, [Nuijten et al. (2016)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26497820/) found at least one inconsistent statistical result in roughly half of the papers containing significance tests that they examined. [[Detecting such errors is neither the job of humans nor LLMs - is there no better example? Then maybe just none and simply something like "Yet there are countless examples where even glaring errors slip through."]] AI reviewers promise to reduce the workload while improving the detection of such problems. Whether they can deliver both needs careful evaluation.

Existing evaluations often assess overlap with human reviews or ask people and models to rate the feedback. For example, [Liang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01783) compared GPT-4's comments with human reviews of 4,805 papers and asked 308 researchers about the usefulness of its feedback. A [recent preprint by Fichtl et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03581) combines overlap measures with LLM ratings and comparisons of review scores. These approaches tell us something about the reviews, but favourable ratings or agreement with another reviewer do not establish that an AI reliably identifies consequential errors or helps authors correct them. That is closer to the outcome I care about: making the literature better.

As a step towards evaluating review *quality* more directly, I like the idea of inserting errors into papers and checking whether a reviewer finds them. That gives us something concrete to evaluate, and to compare across approaches. However, this only works when the changes actually create real errors that an (agentic) reviewer should highlight.

Paul Litvak recently published [a benchmark of 100 planted errors across 10 psychology papers](https://www.paullitvak.com/p/how-well-does-ai-peer-review-work). He modified each paper to add 10 errors, and then tested 14 reviewer configurations, including foundation models with different reasoning settings and two commercial review tools. The best individual configuration identified 71 "errors", and pooling the findings across all 14 configurations identified 93. Litvak acknowledges that he developed the benchmark with Claude and did not thoroughly check all 100 errors himself. He released the [manuscripts, annotations, and results](https://github.com/Dawes-Institute/ai-peer-review-benchmark) to invite further work. That openness makes the benchmark potentially useful in supporting the development of better AI reviewers.

The benchmark caught my attention while I was working on a paper-review pipeline that adapts ideas from [coarse](https://github.com/Davidvandijcke/coarse) but makes it more applicable to (experimental) social sciences beyond economics. More on that soon, I hope. [[Add link to WIP repo, maybe just called "For the very curious."]] In initial testing, I was surprised that the agents found rather few of the errors. When digging into the misses, I started to wonder whether some of them should even be considered errors. So I used AI agents (with GPT 6 Astra) to audit Litvak's annotations against the original and modified manuscripts.[^audit] The initial audit classified 42 targets as demonstrable errors, 24 as material reporting gaps, and nine as requiring further evidence; it raised doubts whether the remaining 25 should be classes as errors at all. The first category includes internal reporting contradictions, sometimes substantially narrower than the scientific defect claimed in the annotation. Obviously, these are provisional model judgments, and follow-up critiques (by Fable 5.1 and Gemini 3.8 Flash) disputed some classifications. Nevertheless, I tend to agree that a substantial number of errors are questionable.

Consider paper 10, a study of spelling and sentence processing by [Ulicheva et al. (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2018.09.013). One planted change states that adding 40 verb fillers makes sentence contexts balanced across three lexical categories. The annotation treats this as an error because there are 80 experimental items and 40 fillers. Yet the 80 experimental items cover two categories, nouns and adjectives. Appendix D contains 40 noun contexts, 40 adjective contexts, and 40 verb contexts. The statement about balance is correct when the fillers are included. Excluding fillers from the outcome analyses does not change the balance of contexts presented to participants either. Thus, there is no error to catch here.

Another change in the same paper removes a description of counterbalancing from the main Methods section. The annotation says that removing the description leaves item properties perfectly confounded with condition. However, Appendix D still describes two lists. Items presented in congruent contexts in List 1 appear in incongruent contexts in List 2, and vice versa. The main text also directs readers to that appendix. A reviewer might reasonably ask how participants were allocated to the lists, and expect more clarity in the Methods, but the manuscript arguably still implies counterbalancing and certainly does not indicate the absence asserted by the answer key. Deleting the description from the Methods did not delete the information from the paper. Both examples can be checked against the [modified paper and its appendices](https://github.com/Dawes-Institute/ai-peer-review-benchmark/blob/3d9188343eebd4312d3bfbde6822cfa4eaf32fb4/modified%20papers/10.docx) and the [annotation key](https://github.com/Dawes-Institute/ai-peer-review-benchmark/blob/3d9188343eebd4312d3bfbde6822cfa4eaf32fb4/error_insertions.csv).

Neither of these "errors" is among the seven that no system matched. Furthermore, Litvak acknowledges that he did not measure false positives that might actually introduce new errors into revisions. Yet, do the seven consistent misses at least clearly show limits to automated review? Not quite.

One is the deletion of a mean-centring statement from paper 5, a replication meta-analysis of self-esteem and perceptions of Facebook by [Leighton et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.24839/2325-7342.JN23.2.98). The annotation claims that without centring, regression coefficients cannot be compared across replications. However, the paper identifies its analyses as simple linear regressions and reports standardised coefficients and correlations. With an intercept in the model, subtracting a constant from the sole predictor changes the intercept but leaves the slope, standardised coefficient, and correlation unchanged. The deletion therefore does not establish the claimed problem with comparability. The [table note and regression footnote](https://github.com/Dawes-Institute/ai-peer-review-benchmark/blob/3d9188343eebd4312d3bfbde6822cfa4eaf32fb4/modified%20papers/5.docx) specify the reported coefficients and simple-regression models. So in this case, there is *no*  (mean-centring) error to be found. 

Another, in paper 7, removes a sentence explaining why an increase in memory errors with a longer delay supports a memory-based explanation. The preceding sentence still explains that purely perceptual errors should not increase with delay, and the argument recurs in the discussion. Treating the absence of an explicit statement that is clearly implied as an "error" really stretches that concept.

The seven targets that no configuration matched are summarised below. My interpretations concern the modified benchmark manuscripts. Overall, I do not see *any* of them as clear errors, save a minor reporting inconsistency in Paper 5. Missing information can warrant criticism. If a paper makes an inference that depends on an unexplained analytical decision, asking for that decision to be reported is useful peer review. When the answer is implied but not explicit, that tends to give rise to a back and forth between reviewers and authors. Real "errors" are a different beast, and they are what I hoped Litvak's benchmark would measure.

| Target | Planted change | Annotation's claim | My interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Paper 2, error 10 | Truncate an open-science disclosure. | Unreported exclusions or measures may be concealed. | Other disclosures and preregistration information survive. The removed assurance might warrant a reporting query; concealed conduct is not established. |
| Paper 4, error 4 | Change an expectation of no temporal change to an expectation of change. | Comparison trajectories must be flat for the slope comparison to work. | Flat slopes are unnecessary. The altered theoretical expectation may need justification or reconciliation with other prose. |
| Paper 5, error 3 | Delete the mean-centring statement. | Regression effects become incomparable. | Centring leaves the relevant simple-regression slopes and correlations unchanged. |
| Paper 5, error 5 | Remove the Midwest from the description of institutional locations. | The description misrepresents institutional diversity. | The table still lists Midwest institutions: a real descriptive inconsistency, but no demonstrated effect on the results. |
| Paper 5, error 6 | Remove detail about adapting an effect-size comparison method to correlations. | The method can no longer be evaluated. | A clarification may help, but the method description, citations, and syntax link remain. |
| Paper 7, error 4 | Delete a sentence explaining the temporal inference. | The argument connecting delay to memory processes is broken. | The premise remains immediately before the deletion, and the argument is repeated elsewhere. |
| Paper 10, error 10 | Lower the upper fixation cutoff from 1,500 to 800 ms. | Differential trimming attenuates the congruency effect. | A sensitivity check is reasonable; the asserted attenuation is not demonstrated. |


Taken together, these examples leave me cautious about interpreting the seven misses as a clear limit on AI reviewers' ability to detect errors. As a post-hoc sensitivity check, restricting the original matches to the 42 targets that the initial Astra audit classified as demonstrable errors gives 39/42 (92.9%) for GPT-5.5 without reasoning, the best configuration on that subset. The original overall winner, GPT-5.5 with high reasoning, matches 38/42 (90.5%); pooling all configurations gives 41/42 (97.6%).[^subset] Human experts might well classify the "errors" differently. Notably, many of the errors that passed review are inconsistencies within the papers, and might thus be easier to detect for models. Nevertheless, these figures therefore show how much the conclusion depends on the definition of errors, and the resolution of disagreements abotu what counts as one.

What could a better benchmark look like? For a given manuscript, we would need plausible potential errors that render the reported results unreliable. These would then need to be inserted consistently across the manuscript, so that the task tests recognition of the methodological problem rather spotting an inconsistency (or failing detection because information is only partly removed). LLMs can likely be useful in generating candidate errors, but these should then be reviewed by both independent LLMs and human experts. To evaluate review ability more broadly, it would also be helpful to add reporting gaps, inconsistencies, deviations from established good practice, and areas that lack clarity. These are valuable additional targets, but they should be distinguished from errors that threaten results. An evaluation should then also assess parsimony and false positives. A reviewer that indiscriminately flags all possible issues slows down science while a reviewer that makes erroneous claims might make science worse. Neither is rescued by high recall.

To be clear: Even though I doubt that the current benchmark developed by Litvak is reliable enough to test AI reviewers, I appreciate the openness and the step towards empirical evaluation. The next step is to make the planted errors reliable enough that failing to flag one tells us something about the reviewer. Before asking why an AI missed an error, we need to establish that there was an error to find.
