Here are eight names for a modular, independent manuscript assessment pipeline designed for dual-author-and-reviewer use. Each avoids the pitfalls you identified and aims for neutral, precise credibility.

---

### 1. **Crosscheck**
**Rationale:** Evokes verification and comparison—like a proofreader’s double check—without implying coaching or gatekeeping. “Cross” suggests independent perspective; “check” is routine, not adversarial.
**Dual-use descriptor:** “Independent evidence-grounded critique for manuscript refinement and peer evaluation.”
**Downside:** May sound too generic or like a simple plagiarism tool (e.g., iThenticate). Needs strong branding to differentiate.

### 2. **VeriScope**
**Rationale:** “Veri-” (truth, evidence) + “-scope” (view, lens). Suggests a systematic, evidence-based viewing of a manuscript’s claims, not a fix-it service. Neutral enough for both author and reviewer.
**Dual-use descriptor:** “Modular verification framework for assessing manuscript arguments and evidence.”
**Downside:** Slightly abstract; might be mistaken for a data-analysis tool. Requires clear explanation of the pipeline.

### 3. **BenchProof**
**Rationale:** “Bench” (standard, reference point) + “Proof” (verification, evidence). Implies testing against a benchmark, not coaching. Works for authors seeking to meet disciplinary standards and reviewers needing a rigorous baseline.
**Dual-use descriptor:** “Standard-based evidence verification for manuscript rigor and reviewer preparation.”
**Downside:** Could sound like a quality control or manufacturing term. Might feel too technical for humanities fields.

### 4. **FaultFind**
**Rationale:** Direct and honest—identifies issues without sugarcoating. “Find” is neutral investigation, not “fix.” Works equally for self-correction and external critique.
**Dual-use descriptor:** “Systematic identification of evidence gaps and argument flaws for revision or evaluation.”
**Downside:** The word “fault” may feel negative or confrontational to some authors. Needs careful framing as constructive, not punitive.

### 5. **CiteCheck**
**Rationale:** Focuses on the backbone of evidence: citations and their support. Modular—can be expanded to other checks. “Check” is neutral, not developmental. Useful for both author and reviewer.
**Dual-use descriptor:** “Evidence-source verification and claim-support analysis for manuscript and peer review.”
**Downside:** Narrowly implies only citation accuracy. Must market as a starting point for broader evidence critique.

### 6. **Grain**
**Rationale:** A fresh take on your earlier “GrainReview.” “Grain” as in granular analysis—examining the texture of an argument, not polishing it. Neutral, evocative, and discipline-agnostic.
**Dual-use descriptor:** “Granular evidence assessment for manuscript refinement and independent review.”
**Downside:** Very short; may need a subtitle for clarity. Risk of sounding like a coffee or agriculture brand.

### 7. **ArgueProof**
**Rationale:** Blends “argument” and “proof.” Focuses on the logical structure of claims and their supporting evidence. Neither coaching nor gatekeeping—just verification.
**Dual-use descriptor:** “Argument and evidence verification for manuscript revision and peer evaluation.”
**Downside:** Slightly awkward blend; might be misread as “argue” in a combative sense. Needs careful phonetic clarity.

### 8. **Trial**
**Rationale:** Like a test or trial run—not a final judgment. Implies iterative testing of claims. Works for authors testing their own work and reviewers testing others’. Simple, memorable, and neutral.
**Dual-use descriptor:** “Modular evidence-testing pipeline for manuscript improvement and reviewer preparation.”
**Downside:** Very broad; could be confused with legal or medical trials. Needs strong contextual branding (e.g., “Manuscript Trial”).

---

## Ranked Top 3 & Tradeoffs

1. **Crosscheck** – Most neutral, direct, and dual-use. The tradeoff is generic risk; it requires clear differentiation from plagiarism checkers. Strongest for both author and reviewer because “check” is the lowest-common-denominator action.

2. **FaultFind** – Most honest and modular. Tradeoff: negative connotation for some authors. Best for users who want direct, no-sugar critique, but may scare away those seeking gentler feedback.

3. **VeriScope** – Most precise and evidence-focused. Tradeoff: abstractness. Best for fields where evidence verification is paramount (STEM, social sciences), but less intuitive for arts/humanities. Strongest for reviewer use; slightly weaker for author self-improvement.

**Key tradeoff across all:** The more neutral and verification-focused the name, the less it signals “helpfulness” to authors—but the more it suits reviewers. The more development-oriented, the more it risks alienating reviewer use. **Crosscheck** balances this best by being action-agnostic (you check, not fix).
