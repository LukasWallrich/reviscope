Literature survey for ReviScope evaluation design (2026-10-08). 47 bibliography entries; 42 opened and checked against an abstract, full text or official page; 5 partly verified (#29, #37, #44, #47 content; #35 venue) and marked as such. Numbers are from the opened sources. Items marked [unverified] or "partially verified" could not be opened directly (bot protection), and points marked [interpretation] are my inferences.

## (a) Proposed multi-dimensional review-quality framework for ReviScope

### Design principles taken from the literature

1. **State the objective first.** Quality can only be judged against agreed objectives [39]. ARCADIA's definition is a good default: a review is good to the extent that it "helps editors make a fair decision and authors improve" [5]. That gives two audiences, editor and author, and ReviScope should be scored for both. Error detection is only one means to these ends.
2. **Score criticisms separately from reports.** Older instruments (RQI [1], ARCADIA [5], editor scales [10], Delphi dimensions [40]) score the whole report: did it cover importance, originality, methods, presentation and interpretation, and was it constructive and substantiated? NLP work scores single review points: actionability, grounding, verifiability and helpfulness [28]; substantiation [25]; deficiency [26]; lazy heuristics [27]; argument type [22]. ReviScope needs both. Criticism-level scores feed precision, correctness and remedy quality. Report-level scores capture coverage, prioritisation, burden and decision usefulness.
3. **Utility rubrics leave out correctness.** RQI, RevUtil, ASAP and the Delphi dimensions measure whether comments look useful: specific, actionable, substantiated and polite. None checks whether a comment is true of the manuscript [1, 28, 40]. LLM reviews have about twice the share of deficient segments that human reviews have [26]. LLM output is also the kind of text that scores well on these surface rubrics. So correctness and materiality must be judged separately, with the manuscript in hand.
4. **Do not validate against one human review or against verdicts.** Inter-reviewer reliability is low: mean ICC .34 and kappa .17 [11]. NeurIPS committees disagreed on 23% of decisions [18]. Two human reviewers share only about 29-35% of their points [20]. Precision therefore cannot be "matched to a human point". The reference set has to be the pooled set of distinct criticisms from all humans and systems, each adjudicated on its merits. This is what ReviScope's planned clustering does.
5. **Ratings of reviews are as noisy and biased as ratings of papers.** Two evaluations of the same review disagree 28-32% of the time [19]. Raters prefer longer reviews even when the extra text is empty [19]. Authors favour reviews that recommend acceptance [3, 19, 38]. LLM judges prefer their own outputs [35] and are poor at spotting deficient review segments (best F1 about 22, recall above precision) [26]. Author and editor ratings do not correlate [38].

### Criticism-level dimensions (unit = one distinct criticism after deduplication, linked to quote(s))

| # | Dimension | Operational definition (suggested coding) | Grounding | Who can rate it reliably | Known biases and risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | **Correctness / validity** | Is the stated problem actually present in the manuscript, and is the reasoning sound? Codes: supported / contradicted (misreading, neglects stated info, wrong statistics) / unresolved (needs data, code or external facts) / not assessable. | Accuracy desideratum [23]; misunderstanding and neglecting stated information are the top human deficiencies [26]; COPE "unfounded accusations" [42]; planted-error studies [6-9]; SPOT precision [32] | Domain/method experts with the full manuscript and supplementary material, blind to source. For numerical claims, a deterministic recomputation where possible. Author responses help as evidence (concede/dispute, [24]) but authors are a biased source [3, 19, 38]. | LLM judges have low precision on this [26]. Planted-error keys are heterogeneous (ReviScope audit). Experts may defer to confident prose. Use at least 2 adjudicators plus a reconciliation step and report kappa. |
| C2 | **Materiality / consequence** | If true, how far does it affect the paper's main claims? Ordinal: (3) undermines a primary claim or conclusion; (2) weakens a primary claim or affects a secondary claim; (1) local or reporting issue with no inferential effect; (0) cosmetic or preference. Optionally map to eLife strength-of-evidence terms (solid / incomplete / inadequate) [43]. | Major vs minor errors [8, 9, 7]; authors rate "focus on important aspects" lowest [38]; GPT-4 mainly catches points several humans raised [20]; eLife separates significance from evidence [43] | Experts. Expect only moderate reliability, so anchor with a vignette per level. Points raised independently by several reviewers or systems are a cheap proxy for materiality [20]. | Severity inflation by both LLM and human reviewers. Training made reviewers reject more [8], so stricter is not the same as better. Raters may confuse "hard to fix" with "important". |
| C3 | **Grounding & specificity** | Does the point identify where the problem is (section, table, quote, statistic) and exactly what is wrong? 1-5 scale as in RevUtil [28]. | RevUtil [28]; vague comments were a target of the ICLR feedback agent [21]; MARG "generic" rate [30]; COPE "be specific" [42] | Can be largely automated: deterministic quote-anchor checks, then an LLM or crowd rating. Human alpha is only about .39 [28], so do not over-invest in human rating here. | Specific-looking but wrong points score well, so C3 must always be read alongside C1. |
| C4 | **Substantiation / verifiability** | Is the claim backed by reasoning, a calculation, a citation or a quote that a reader can check? Is there a claim at all? (RevUtil binary claim check, then 1-5 [28].) | RQI item 7 [1]; SubstanReview [25]; COPE "provide supporting evidence" [42] | Trained crowd or LLM with spot-checks (alpha about .46 [28]). Whether the cited evidence actually supports the claim belongs under C1 (expert). | Fabricated or irrelevant citations look like substantiation. Verify references the system cites. |
| C5 | **Remedy quality and proportionality** | (a) Actionability: is a concrete fix given (1-5 [28])? (b) Necessity: is the remedy *essential* to support the claims as stated, does it only *strengthen*, or does it *extend* the work beyond scope [42]? (c) Proportionality: is the remedy the least burdensome one that resolves the problem (e.g. "soften the claim" versus "collect new data")? (d) Correctness of the remedy itself: would the suggested analysis be valid? | COPE essential/strengthen/extend [42]; reviewer-requested unplanned analyses harmed reports [14]; out-of-scope experiments are LLMs' top deficiency (30.49%) [26]; "extra experiments" is the most frequent lazy heuristic [27]; APA constructive/actionable [47] | Actionability: crowd or authors (alpha about .56, the most reliable RevUtil aspect [28]). Necessity and proportionality: experts or the handling editor. | Raters reward more and longer requests [19]. Authors underrate necessary but costly requests [38]. LLMs over-request [26]. |
| C6 | **Calibration / epistemic honesty** | Does the stated confidence or label (finding vs unresolved concern) match the adjudicated status? Does the review say what it did not or could not check? | COPE: indicate which aspects were not assessed [42]; SPOT: low confidence and poor run-to-run agreement [32] | Computed, not rated: compare ReviScope's verifier label or confidence with the C1 adjudication (Brier score or reliability diagram). | Needs enough adjudicated items per confidence bin, so plan for at least several hundred criticisms across papers. |
| C7 | **Tone / professionalism** | Free of hostile, derogatory or accusatory language. Accusations of misconduct or QRPs are made only with evidence. Respectful phrasing (third person, "the manuscript") [42, 47]. | PolitePEER 5-level scale [29]; harm to under-represented groups [16]; ICLR agent flagged unprofessional remarks [21] | Automatable with LLM or classifier plus a light human audit. | LLMs are usually polite, so the real risk is *overstated certainty* (C6) and unfounded integrity insinuations, not rudeness [interpretation]. |

### Report-level dimensions (unit = whole ReviScope report for one manuscript)

| # | Dimension | Operational definition | Grounding | Who rates | Biases and risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1 | **Coverage / focus** | Which aspects did the report address substantively? Use RQI/ARCADIA domains (importance/contribution; robustness of methods; statistics; interpretation and discussion; reporting and transparency; presentation) or the ASAP aspects (motivation, originality, soundness, substance, replicability, comparison, clarity). Report this as a distribution, following the "focus" approach [31]. Also measure **pooled recall**: the share of adjudicated-valid, material (C2 ≥ 2) criticisms from the pooled human+system set that this report raised. | RQI [1]; ARCADIA [5]; Superchi review (statistics covered by only 1/18 tools) [4]; ASAP [23]; focus framework [31] | Automatable from the criticism clusters once C1/C2 adjudication exists. | Coverage is cheap: generated reviews cover more aspects but are less constructive and less factual [23]. Never reward coverage without C1/C2. LLMs over-focus on technical validity and neglect novelty [31]. For ReviScope this may be acceptable, but say so. |
| R2 | **Prioritisation / triage** | Are the most material issues first and clearly separated from minor ones? Are essential and optional remedies distinguished [42]? Metric: rank correlation between report order or major/minor labels and the adjudicated C2. | Authors find "focus on important aspects" weakest [38]; COPE [42]; Delphi "Relevance" [40] | Computed from C2 plus report structure; editors can rate it holistically. | Length and number of issues can mask poor triage. |
| R3 | **Signal-to-noise and author burden** | Share of criticisms that are incorrect (C1), immaterial (C2 = 0), or carry unnecessary or out-of-scope remedies (C5b). Also the count of requested actions, especially new data or analyses, and words per material valid point. | Out-of-scope and unplanned-analysis harms [14, 26, 27]; COPE "not to extend the work beyond its current scope" [42]; length bias [19] | Computed from the criticism-level codes. | Easy to reward long reports. Always report this next to R1 so the precision/recall trade-off is visible. |
| R4 | **Decision usefulness and congruence** | Does the summary give a clear, separately justified judgement of (a) importance/contribution and (b) strength of evidence, consistent with the listed findings? Score whether the overall judgement is congruent with the findings, not whether it agrees with the eventual editorial decision. | Decisiveness desideratum [23]; COPE congruence [42]; eLife two-axis vocabulary [43]; PCI RR and Meta-Psychology drop novelty [44, 45]; PLOS soundness-only criteria [46] | Editors or experts. | Verdict agreement is a poor target: kappa .17 [11], 23% committee disagreement [18], subjective variance about 50% [17]. |
| R5 | **Stability / reproducibility** | Agreement of the material, valid criticisms across repeated runs with the same configuration (e.g. Jaccard overlap of adjudicated clusters). | SPOT: models rarely rediscover the same errors across 8 runs [32]; reproducibility concern [34] | Computed. | Needs at least 3 runs per configuration. The current one-run-per-config evaluation cannot separate stage effects from run noise [interpretation]. |
| R6 | **Distinctiveness / homogenisation (corpus level)** | Across papers, do reports reuse generic templates (e.g. "add a power analysis", "discuss limitations of self-report")? Measure the rate of generic points (MARG's "generic" criterion [30]) and the diversity of criticisms across papers. | Generic comments [30]; LLM-modified reviews at scale [33]; epistemic-community risk [34] | Computed plus a small human audit. | Generic points are often true but low-value, so they inflate apparent precision. |

### Rater assignment summary
- **Experts (blind to source, manuscript in hand, at least 2 per item, with reconciliation):** C1, C2, C5b-d, R4. Use them on deduplicated clusters, not on whole reports, to keep the cost manageable.
- **Crowd / trained non-experts or authors:** C3, C4, C5a (actionability), C7. RevUtil alphas of .39-.56 [28] show the expected ceiling.
- **LLM judges:** acceptable for C3, C4, C7 and for screening, if they are calibrated against a human-labelled subset and come from a different model family than the generator [35]. Not acceptable as the sole judge of C1/C2 [26]. Length-normalise or length-match any pairwise comparison [19].
- **Computed:** R1-R3, R5, R6 and C6, all derived from the criticism-level codes.

### How this maps to ReviScope's open questions
- *Precision:* precision = share of a system's criticisms adjudicated as supported (C1) and material (C2 ≥ 1), estimated on a random sample of unmatched findings. An unmatched finding is not presumed wrong, because human-human overlap is only about 30% [20].
- *Non-error contributions:* score interpretation, theory and presentation points on the same C1 (is the critique valid?), C2 and C5 scales. R1 makes their presence visible.
- *Whether stages earn their cost:* compare configurations on (valid and material criticisms found) minus a penalty for (incorrect plus unnecessary-remedy criticisms) per paper, with R5 run-to-run variance as the noise floor.

## (b) Takeaways

1. **No validated gold-standard instrument exists.** Superchi et al. found 24 tools, none defining "quality", and only 1 with documented scale development [4]. ARCADIA [5] and the 2026 editor Delphi [40] are the most principled recent options. Borrow their dimensions, not a single score.
2. **The best-validated human instruments rate coverage and manner, not correctness.** The RQI asks whether the reviewer commented on importance, originality, methods, presentation and interpretation, and whether comments were constructive and substantiated [1]. ReviScope must add a correctness adjudication layer.
3. **Human error detection is low, so it is a modest benchmark.** BMJ reviewers found about 2.6 of 9 inserted major errors [9]. Reviewers commented on about 2 of 8 weaknesses [6] and found 17-39% of major errors depending on their recommendation [7]. Short training helps only slightly and briefly [8, 9]. An AI reviewer finding about 30-60% of planted major errors is in or above the human range. Recall on planted errors is still only one narrow facet of quality.
4. **Agreement with "the human reviewer" is a weak criterion.** IRR kappa is about .17 [11]. Committees disagree on 23% of decisions [18]. Two humans share only about 30% of their points [20]. Use pooled, adjudicated criticism sets instead.
5. **Ratings of reviews have the same pathologies as ratings of papers.** Two evaluations of the same review disagree 28-32% of the time. Padding a review with empty text raises its quality ratings. Authors favour reviews that recommend acceptance [19]. Length-match or length-normalise all pairwise judgements.
6. **LLM judges are risky for correctness.** The best LLM found deficient review segments with F1 about 22, with more false alarms than misses [26]. Self-preference is documented [35]. Use cross-family judges for surface dimensions only, and calibrate them against human labels.
7. **Point-level utility can be measured with moderate reliability.** RevUtil's actionability, grounding/specificity, verifiability and helpfulness reach Krippendorff alpha .39-.56 [28]. Machine reviews scored below human reviews on all four. The rubric ignores correctness.
8. **The characteristic failure of LLM reviewers is unnecessary or out-of-scope requests,** not rudeness: 30.49% of deficient LLM segments [26]. "Authors should do extra experiments" is the most frequent lazy heuristic among humans too [27]. Unplanned extra analyses requested by reviewers made published reports worse [14]. Remedy necessity and proportionality deserve their own dimension.
9. **COPE gives a citable norm for proportionality.** Reviewers should say "which (if any) suggested additional investigations are essential to support claims ... and which will just strengthen or extend the work", should not extend the work beyond its scope, and should state what they did not assess [42].
10. **Materiality and prioritisation are what authors most often miss.** Authors rated "focus on important aspects" lowest [38]. GPT-4 mostly catches points that several humans raised [20]. Multi-source convergence is a cheap proxy for materiality; expert C2 coding is the real measure.
11. **Coverage is cheap and must not be rewarded on its own.** Generated reviews cover more aspects than humans but are less constructive and less factual [23]. LLMs over-focus on technical validity and neglect novelty [31].
12. **Separate "importance" from "strength of evidence".** eLife uses two separate vocabularies [43]. Open-science venues relevant to social psychology (PCI RR, Meta-Psychology) exclude novelty from the reviewer's remit [44, 45], and Meta-Psychology adds analytic reproduction [45]. Evaluate ReviScope's methodological findings on the evidence axis, and its contribution and theory comments on their own axis.
13. **Statistical and methodological checking is a recognised and under-supplied function.** It is where specialist modules are most defensible. Reviewers trained in statistics or epidemiology produced better reviews [2]. Statistical review is rarely used and valued by editors [15]. Adding a statistical or reporting-guideline reviewer improved final manuscripts modestly [12, 13].
14. **Downstream outcomes are possible but need large samples.** Improvements in the revised manuscript [12, 13, 14] and reviewer or author uptake [21] are valid end-points. Effects are small (e.g. +0.25 on a 5-point scale [13]), so pilots with a few papers can only estimate feasibility.
15. **Stability and homogenisation are AI-specific quality dimensions** with little precedent in the human literature. Models rarely rediscover the same errors across runs [32], and a corpus of LLM reviews can drift toward generic templates [30, 33, 34]. Measure run-to-run agreement and the rate of generic points.

## (c) Annotated bibliography

Each entry gives the full citation, URL and verification route, followed by the relevance note.

### A. Instruments for assessing review-report quality

1. **van Rooyen S, Black N, Godlee F. (1999).** Development of the review quality instrument (RQI) for assessing peer reviews of manuscripts. *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*, 52(7), 625-629. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0895-4356(99)00047-5 (PubMed 10391655). Verified via Europe PMC abstract.
   - 7 items, each 1-5: whether the reviewer commented on (1) importance of the question, (2) originality, (3) strengths/weaknesses of method, (4) presentation, (5) interpretation of results; plus two properties of the review itself: (6) constructiveness, (7) substantiation of comments. Alpha 0.84; inter-rater Kw 0.83 (editors). The RQI is a *coverage + manner* instrument at report level; it does not assess whether the comments are correct.

2. **Black N, van Rooyen S, Godlee F, Smith R, Evans S. (1998).** What makes a good reviewer and a good review for a general medical journal? *JAMA*, 280(3), 231-233. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.280.3.231. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - 690 BMJ reviews rated by 2 editors and the corresponding author using the 7-item RQI. Reviewer characteristics explained only 8% of quality variance; training in epidemiology/statistics was the only factor associated with higher quality by both editors and authors; quality rose with time spent up to ~3 h but not beyond. Shows editors and authors can both serve as raters but with different perspectives.

3. **van Rooyen S, Godlee F, Evans S, Smith R, Black N. (1999).** Effect of blinding and unmasking on the quality of peer review. *Journal of General Internal Medicine*, 14(10), 622-624. https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1525-1497.1999.09058.x. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - 527 BMJ manuscripts; review quality rated by two editors and the corresponding author: no difference between editors' and authors' assessments overall, but **reviews recommending publication were scored more highly than those recommending rejection** -- a known valence bias when the reviewed party rates the review.

4. **Superchi C, González JA, Solà I, Cobo E, Hren D, Boutron I. (2019).** Tools used to assess the quality of peer review reports: a methodological systematic review. *BMC Medical Research Methodology*, 19, 48. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-019-0688-x. Verified (Europe PMC abstract).
   - 24 tools (23 scales, 1 checklist); none defined "quality"; only 1 described scale development; 10 reported validity/reliability. 9 quality domains/11 subdomains; "detailed/thorough" comments in 11/18 multi-item tools, comments on statistical methods in only 1/18. Conclusion: concepts vary widely and validation is questionable -- i.e. there is no gold-standard review-quality measure to borrow wholesale.

5. **Superchi C, Hren D, Blanco D, Rius R, Recchioni A, Boutron I, González JA. (2020).** Development of ARCADIA: a tool for assessing the quality of peer-review reports in biomedical research. *BMJ Open*, 10(6), e035604. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2019-035604. Verified (Europe PMC abstract).
   - Defines review-report quality as "the extent to which a peer-review report helps editors make a fair decision and authors improve" (84% of 446 editors/authors endorsed). Final checklist: 14 items in 5 domains -- Importance of the study; Robustness of the study methods; Interpretation and discussion of results; Reporting and transparency; Characteristics of the reviewer's comments. The dual-audience (editor decision + author improvement) definition is directly useful for ReviScope.

### B. Empirical studies of human review quality, error detection, training

6. **Godlee F, Gale CR, Martyn CN. (1998).** Effect on the quality of peer review of blinding reviewers and asking them to sign their reports: a randomized controlled trial. *JAMA*, 280(3), 237-240. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.280.3.237. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - Accepted paper modified to contain 8 areas of weakness; 221 reviewers returned reports; mean number of weaknesses commented on was about 2 of 8, irrespective of blinding/signing. Early planted-weakness design.

7. **Baxt WG, Waeckerle JF, Berlin JA, Callaham ML. (1998).** Who reviews the reviewers? Feasibility of using a fictitious manuscript to evaluate peer reviewer performance. *Annals of Emergency Medicine*, 32(3), 310-317. https://doi.org/10.1016/s0196-0644(98)70006-x. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - Fictitious RCT with 10 major and 13 minor errors sent to 262 reviewers (203 returned). Reviewers recommending rejection found 39.1% of major errors; those recommending acceptance 17.3%. Error detection covaries with recommendation -- recall on planted errors is a partial and decision-confounded quality measure.

8. **Schroter S, Black N, Evans S, Carpenter J, Godlee F, Smith R. (2004).** Effects of training on quality of peer review: randomised controlled trial. *BMJ*, 328, 673. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.38023.700775.ae. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - BMJ reviewers; outcomes: RQI score, number of deliberate major errors identified, time, rejection rate. Self-taught training raised RQI 2.85 vs 2.56 (not "of editorial significance", not maintained); major errors found 3.14/2.96 vs 2.13 after training; effects faded by 6 months; training increased rejection recommendations (92%/84% vs 76%).

9. **Schroter S, Black N, Evans S, Godlee F, Osorio L, Smith R. (2008).** What errors do peer reviewers detect, and does training improve their ability to detect them? *Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine*, 101(10), 507-514. https://doi.org/10.1258/jrsm.2008.080062. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - 607 BMJ reviewers; three test papers each with 9 major + 5 minor inserted errors. At baseline reviewers found on average 2.58/9 major errors (2.71 and 3.0 in later papers). Biased randomisation was most often detected. "Editors should not assume that reviewers will detect most major errors, particularly those concerned with the context of study." Baseline human recall on planted errors is ~30%.

10. **Callaham ML, Tercier J. (2007).** The relationship of previous training and experience of journal peer reviewers to subsequent review quality. *PLoS Medicine*, 4(1), e40. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.0040040. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - 2,856 reviews editor-rated on a standardised quality scale; formal training in critical appraisal/statistics, academic rank etc. failed to predict quality; predictors weak overall. Editor quality ratings are a feasible routine report-level outcome.

11. **Bornmann L, Mutz R, Daniel HD. (2010).** A reliability-generalization study of journal peer reviews: a multilevel meta-analysis of inter-rater reliability and its determinants. *PLoS ONE*, 5(12), e14331. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0014331. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - 70 coefficients from 48 studies (19,443 manuscripts): mean ICC/r^2 = .34, mean Cohen's kappa = .17. Larger studies report lower IRR. Implication: agreement with "the human reviewer" on overall recommendation is a weak target; low inter-reviewer agreement is the norm.

12. **Cobo E, Selva-O'Callagham A, Ribera JM, Cardellach F, Dominguez R, Vilardell M. (2007).** Statistical reviewers improve reporting in biomedical articles: a randomized trial. *PLoS ONE*, 2(3), e332. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0000332. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - 2x2 factorial RCT (adding a statistical reviewer; suggesting reporting checklists) at Medicina Clínica, 129 manuscripts; outcome = change in manuscript quality (Goodman scale) from submission to final version. Uses *manuscript improvement* as the outcome rather than rating the review itself.

13. **Cobo E, Cortés J, Ribera JM, et al. (2011).** Effect of using reporting guidelines during peer review on quality of final manuscripts submitted to a biomedical journal: masked randomised trial. *BMJ*, 343, d6783. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.d6783. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - Additional reporting-guideline-based review improved final manuscript quality modestly (as allocated 0.25 on 5-point scale, 95% CI -0.05 to 0.54; as reviewed 0.33, 0.03 to 0.63). Downstream manuscript improvement as an outcome; small effects, large n needed.

14. **Hopewell S, Collins GS, Boutron I, Yu LM, Cook J, Shanyinde M, Wharton R, Shamseer L, Altman DG. (2014).** Impact of peer review on reports of randomised trials published in open peer review journals: retrospective before and after study. *BMJ*, 349, g4145. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.g4145. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - 93 RCT reports in BMC journals; compared submitted vs published versions and reviewer reports. Most reviewer-requested changes improved reporting (e.g. toning down conclusions, n=27), but **some requests had a negative impact, such as adding unplanned analyses (n=15)**; reviewers also failed to detect important reporting deficiencies. Direct evidence that remedies/requests can cause harm -- a model for coding "necessity/appropriateness of remedy" and for using open review histories (as ReviScope does).

15. **Hardwicke TE, Goodman SN. (2020).** How often do leading biomedical journals use statistical experts to evaluate statistical methods? The results of a survey. *PLoS ONE*, 15(10), e0239598. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0239598. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - 107 journals: 34% rarely/never use specialised statistical review; 23% use it for all articles; editors regard statistical review as having substantial incremental value. Supports treating statistical/method checking as a distinct, under-supplied review function where automated help is most defensible.

16. **Silbiger NJ, Stubler AD. (2019).** Unprofessional peer reviews disproportionately harm underrepresented groups in STEM. *PeerJ*, 7, e8247. https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.8247. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - Survey: unprofessional comments were received equally across groups but underrepresented groups were most likely to report negative effects on perceived aptitude, productivity and career. Grounds a "tone/professionalism" harm dimension.

### C. Consistency experiments and reviews-of-reviews (ML conferences)

17. **Cortes C, Lawrence ND. (2021).** Inconsistency in Conference Peer Review: Revisiting the 2014 NeurIPS Experiment. arXiv:2109.09774. https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.09774. Verified (arXiv).
   - 2014 NeurIPS duplicate-committee experiment: ~50% of variation in reviewer quality scores was subjective; for accepted papers, scores did not correlate with later citations; process good at identifying poor papers, poor at identifying good ones. Warns against removing the subjective element.

18. **Beygelzimer A, Dauphin YN, Liang P, Wortman Vaughan J. (2023).** Has the Machine Learning Review Process Become More Arbitrary as the Field Has Grown? The NeurIPS 2021 Consistency Experiment. arXiv:2306.03262. https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.03262. Verified (arXiv).
   - 10% of submissions reviewed by two independent committees: disagreement on accept/reject for 23% of papers; roughly half the accepted list would change on rerun. Overall verdicts are noisy; ReviScope should not be validated against accept/reject decisions or overall scores.

19. **Goldberg A, Stelmakh I, Cho K, Oh A, Agarwal A, Belgrave D, Shah NB. (2025).** Peer reviews of peer reviews: A randomized controlled trial and other experiments. *PLoS ONE*, 20(4), e0320444. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0320444 (arXiv:2311.09497). Verified (Europe PMC + arXiv).
   - NeurIPS 2022: (meta-)reviewers and authors rated reviews. RCT: reviews artificially **lengthened with non-informative content were rated significantly higher quality** (length bias). Authors are positively biased toward reviews recommending acceptance of their own papers. Disagreement between two evaluations of the same review 28-32% -- comparable to paper reviewing itself; miscalibration and criteria-to-overall mapping variability also similar. Key warning for any human or LLM judge of ReviScope reports: control for length and valence; expect review-quality ratings to be as noisy as paper ratings.

20. **Liang W, Zhang Y, Cao H, Wang B, Ding D, Yang X, Vodrahalli K, He S, Smith D, Yin Y, McFarland D, Zou J. (2024).** Can large language models provide useful feedback on research papers? A large-scale empirical analysis. *NEJM AI*, 1(8). https://doi.org/10.1056/AIoa2400196 (arXiv:2310.01783). Verified (arXiv abstract + Crossref).
   - Overlap of GPT-4 comments with human reviewer comments (30.85% Nature journals, 39.23% ICLR) comparable to human-human overlap (28.58%, 35.25%) -- i.e. **human reviewers raise largely non-overlapping points**, so recall against one human's points is an incomplete target and unmatched points are not necessarily wrong. User study (n=308): 57.4% found feedback helpful/very helpful; Users reported vague/non-domain-specific feedback and a lack of "in-depth critique of model architecture and design". GPT-4's hit rate rose with the number of humans who raised a point (Nature: 11.39% for single-reviewer points, 20.67% for two, 31.67% for 3+; ICLR 15.39/26.21/39.33%) -- multi-reviewer consensus points are a natural proxy for materiality/priority.

21. **Thakkar N, Yuksekgonul M, Silberg J, Garg A, Peng N, Sha F, Yu R, Vondrick C, Zou J. (2025).** Can LLM feedback enhance review quality? A randomized study of 20K reviews at ICLR 2025. arXiv:2504.09737. https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.09737. Verified (arXiv).
   - Review Feedback Agent flagged vague comments, content misunderstandings and unprofessional remarks; 27% of reviewers who got feedback updated; updated reviews ~80 words longer and rated more informative by blinded researchers. Uses LLM "reliability tests" as guardrails. Shows vagueness, misunderstanding and unprofessionalism as operational review defects -- but note outcome partly confounded with length (cf. Goldberg et al.).

### D. NLP taxonomies of review content and utility

22. **Hua X, Nikolov M, Badugu N, Wang L. (2019).** Argument Mining for Understanding Peer Reviews. *Proceedings of NAACL-HLT 2019*, 2131-2137. https://aclanthology.org/N19-1219/ (arXiv:1903.10104). Verified (ACL Anthology + arXiv).
   - AMPERE dataset: 400 reviews, 10,386 propositions typed as Evaluation, Request, Fact, Reference, Quote (plus non-argument). Provides a proposition-level unit and the evaluation-vs-request distinction (judgement vs remedy).

23. **Yuan W, Liu P, Neubig G. (2022).** Can We Automate Scientific Reviewing? *Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research*, 75, 171-212. https://doi.org/10.1613/jair.1.12862 (arXiv:2102.00176). Verified (arXiv PDF read; JAIR listing).
   - ASAP-Review: aspect labels Summary, Motivation/Impact, Originality, Soundness/Correctness, Substance, Replicability, Meaningful Comparison, Clarity (with polarity). Desiderata of a good review: decisiveness, comprehensiveness, justification, accuracy, kindness; metrics include aspect coverage, aspect recall vs meta-review, informativeness, aspect-level constructiveness, summary accuracy. Finding: generated reviews cover *more* aspects than humans but are less constructive and less factual -- coverage is cheap, correctness and constructiveness are not.

24. **Kennard N, O'Gorman T, Das R, Sharma A, Bagchi C, Clinton M, Yelugam PK, Zamani H, McCallum A. (2022).** DISAPERE: A Dataset for Discourse Structure in Peer Review Discussions. *Proceedings of NAACL 2022*. https://aclanthology.org/2022.naacl-main.89/ (arXiv:2110.08520). Verified (ACL Anthology + arXiv).
   - 506 review-rebuttal pairs, 20k sentences: review action, aspect, polarity; rebuttal sentences linked to review arguments with author stance (concede, dispute, etc.). Author responses are a usable signal of review-point validity/uptake -- relevant to ReviScope's open-review comparisons (author response letters).

25. **Guo Y, Shang G, Rennard V, Vazirgiannis M, Clavel C. (2023).** Automatic Analysis of Substantiation in Scientific Peer Reviews. *Findings of EMNLP 2023*, 10198-10216. https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-emnlp.684/ (arXiv:2311.11967). Verified (ACL Anthology + arXiv).
   - SubstanReview: 550 NLP reviews annotated with claim-evidence pairs; substantiation = whether claims are supported by evidence. Operationalises the RQI "substantiation" item at claim level.

26. **Du J, Wang Y, Zhao W, et al. (2024).** LLMs Assist NLP Researchers: Critique Paper (Meta-)Reviewing. *Proceedings of EMNLP 2024* (main). arXiv:2406.16253. https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.16253. Verified (arXiv page + HTML).
   - ReviewCritique: expert segment-level "deficiency" labels on human and LLM reviews of NLP submissions. 100% of LLM reviews vs 71.57% of human reviews had >=1 deficient segment; 13.97% vs 6.27% of segments. Human top deficiencies: misunderstanding (22.86%), neglecting stated information (19.64%), inexpert statements (18.23%); LLM top: **suggesting out-of-scope experiments (30.49%)**, misunderstanding, superficial review. LLMs as meta-reviewers detecting deficient segments: best F1 ~22, recall > precision. Directly supports a criticism-level "incorrect / out-of-scope / superficial" harm coding and caution about LLM judges.

27. **Purkayastha S, Li Z, Lauscher A, Qu L, Gurevych I. (2025).** LazyReview: A Dataset for Uncovering Lazy Thinking in NLP Peer Reviews. *Proceedings of ACL 2025*. arXiv:2504.11042. https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.11042. Verified (arXiv HTML).
   - 16 "lazy thinking" heuristics drawn from ACL Rolling Review 2022 and EMNLP 2020 reviewer guidelines (e.g. results not novel/surprising, method too simple, topic too niche, language errors, "authors should do extra experiments", does not beat SOTA, not tested on English). Most frequent: unjustified extra-experiment requests. Annotator kappa rose from 0.31 to 0.52 with guideline refinement. Zero-shot LLMs struggle to detect lazy thinking; feedback made reviews more comprehensive/actionable.

28. **Sadallah A, Baumgärtner T, Gurevych I, Briscoe T. (2025).** The Good, the Bad and the Constructive: Automatically Measuring Peer Review's Utility for Authors. *Proceedings of EMNLP 2025* (main). arXiv:2509.04484. https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04484. Verified (arXiv page + HTML).
   - RevUtil: unit = individual review comment (weakness point). Aspects (1-5): Actionability; Grounding & Specificity; Verifiability (binary claim detection, then 1-5 support via reasoning, knowledge or references); Helpfulness. 1,430 human-labelled comments, 3 annotators: Krippendorff alpha 0.56 (actionability), 0.39 (grounding/specificity), 0.46 (verifiability), 0.47 (helpfulness). Machine-generated reviews underperform human reviews on all four. **Note: none of the aspects assesses whether the comment is correct** -- utility rubrics must be paired with correctness adjudication.

29. **Bharti PK, Navlakha M, Agarwal M, Ekbal A. (2024; online 2023).** PolitePEER: does peer review hurt? A dataset to gauge politeness intensity in the peer reviews. *Language Resources and Evaluation*, 58. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10579-023-09662-3. Verified via Crossref metadata; content (5-level politeness scale from highly impolite to highly polite; sentences from ICLR, NeurIPS, Publons and ShitMyReviewersSay) from search-engine summary of the publisher page -- publisher page not opened (bot-blocked) [details partially unverified].

30. **D'Arcy M, Hope T, Birnbaum L, Downey D. (2024).** MARG: Multi-Agent Review Generation for Scientific Papers. arXiv:2401.04259. https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04259. Verified (arXiv).
   - Multi-agent, specialised sub-tasks (experiments, clarity, impact). User study: GPT-4 baselines produced generic comments >50% of the time and only 1.7 "good" comments per paper; MARG reduced generic comments from 60% to 29% and produced 3.7 good comments per paper. Relevant both as a specialist-module precedent and as an example of evaluating at comment level by authors ("good" = specific + accurate + helpful).

31. **Shin H, Tang J, Lee Y, Kim N, Lim H, Cho JY, Hong H, Lee M, Kim J. (2025).** Mind the Blind Spots: A Focus-Level Evaluation Framework for LLM Reviews. arXiv:2502.17086. https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17086. Verified (arXiv).
   - Represents a review's "focus" as a distribution over targets (problem, method, experiment...) x aspects (validity, clarity, novelty...). Off-the-shelf LLMs over-focus on technical validity and under-assess novelty relative to 676 expert OpenReview reviews. Supports a coverage/focus dimension distinct from correctness.

32. **Son G, Hong J, Fan H, Nam H, Ko H, Lim S, Song J, Choi J, Paulo G, Yu Y, Biderman S. (2025).** When AI Co-Scientists Fail: SPOT -- a Benchmark for Automated Verification of Scientific Research. arXiv:2505.11855. https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.11855. Verified (arXiv).
   - 83 published papers with 91 errata/retraction-level errors. Best LLM <= 21.1% recall and 6.1% precision; across eight runs models rarely rediscover the same errors. Real (non-planted) error benchmark; emphasises precision and run-to-run stability as necessary metrics.

33. **Liang W, Izzo Z, Zhang Y, et al. (2024).** Monitoring AI-Modified Content at Scale: A Case Study on the Impact of ChatGPT on AI Conference Peer Reviews. *Proceedings of ICML 2024* (per arXiv comment). arXiv:2403.07183. https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.07183. Verified (arXiv).
   - 6.5-16.9% of review text at ICLR 2024/NeurIPS 2023/CoRL 2023/EMNLP 2023 estimated substantially LLM-modified; higher in low-confidence, near-deadline reviews from reviewers less likely to respond to rebuttals. Background for homogenisation concerns (corpus-level effects invisible per review).

34. **Hosseini M, Horbach SPJM. (2023).** Fighting reviewer fatigue or amplifying bias? Considerations and recommendations for use of ChatGPT and other large language models in scholarly peer review. *Research Integrity and Peer Review*, 8, 4. https://doi.org/10.1186/s41073-023-00133-5. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - Organised around Tennant & Ross-Hellauer's themes (reviewer role, editor role, functions and quality of reviews, reproducibility, social/epistemic functions). Flags bias, opacity, reproducibility and epistemic-community effects (homogenisation of norms) as risks of LLM reviewing.

35. **Panickssery A, Bowman SR, Feng S. (2024).** LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations. arXiv:2404.13076. https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076. Verified (arXiv). [NeurIPS 2024 venue not verified]

36. **Kuznetsov I, Afzal OM, Dercksen K, et al. (2024).** What Can Natural Language Processing Do for Peer Review? arXiv:2405.06563. https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06563. Verified (arXiv).
   - Multi-author roadmap (incl. Rogers, Shah, Gurevych, Smith): step-by-step map of where NLP can help in peer review and where it "should stand aside"; stresses operationalisation and experimentation challenges and ethics.

37. **Shah NB. (2022).** Challenges, experiments, and computational solutions in peer review. *Communications of the ACM*, 65(6), 76-87. https://doi.org/10.1145/3528086. Verified (Crossref metadata). Overview of problems (e.g. miscalibration, subjectivity, biases) and experiments in peer review [content summary not checked against full text -- partially verified].

38. **Gibson M, Spong CY, Simonsen SE, Martin S, Scott JR. (2008).** Author perception of peer review. *Obstetrics & Gynecology*, 112(3), 646-652. https://doi.org/10.1097/AOG.0b013e31818425d4. Verified (Europe PMC).
   - Authors of accepted manuscripts more satisfied (98% vs 80%). **"The extent to which reviewers focused on important aspects" received the lowest author ratings**; author ratings of reviewer comments did not correlate with senior editors' ratings. 39. **Jefferson T, Wager E, Davidoff F. (2002).** Measuring the quality of editorial peer review. *JAMA*, 287(21), 2786-2790. https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.287.21.2786. Verified (Europe PMC).

### E. Journal / ethics guidance (normative definitions of a good review)

42. **COPE Council. (2017).** Ethical guidelines for peer reviewers (Version 2, September 2017; v1 March 2013 by Irene Hames). Committee on Publication Ethics. https://doi.org/10.24318/cope.2019.1.9 ; PDF copy opened: https://web.sciedupress.com/images/COPE/Ethical_Guidelines_For_Peer_Reviewers_2.pdf. Verified (PDF text read; DOI from search result).
   - "Be objective and constructive... be specific in your critique, and provide supporting evidence with appropriate references to substantiate general statements"; no "hostile or inflammatory" comments or "unfounded accusations"; recommendation "should be congruent with the comments"; indicate which aspects were not assessed; "It is not the job of the reviewer to extend the work beyond its current scope. **Be clear which (if any) suggested additional investigations are essential to support claims made in the manuscript under consideration and which will just strengthen or extend the work**"; no coercive self-citation; do not request unnecessary additional information. This is the clearest normative basis for proportionality/necessity-of-remedy and scope-of-competence disclosure.

43. **eLife. (2022, 20 October).** The eLife Model: What is an eLife assessment? *Inside eLife*. https://elifesciences.org/inside-elife/db24dd46/elife-s-new-model-what-is-an-elife-assessment. Verified (page opened).
   - Two separate controlled vocabularies: **significance of findings** (landmark, fundamental, important, valuable, useful) and **strength of evidence** (exceptional, compelling, convincing, solid, incomplete, inadequate) with definitions (e.g. "solid: methods, data and analyses broadly support the claims with only minor weaknesses"; "incomplete: main claims are only partially supported"). Model for separating importance judgement from evidential judgement in ReviScope's summary.

44. **PCI Registered Reports. (n.d.).** Guide for Reviewers. https://rr.peercommunityin.org/help/guide_for_reviewers. 45. **Meta-Psychology. (n.d.).** About the journal. https://open.lnu.se/index.php/metapsychology/about. Verified (page opened).
