1. Summary

ReviScope's evaluations so far are hard to act on, and the literature suggests that much of the difficulty lies in the measurement. Four outcomes are being read off instruments that each capture one of them partially: recovery of known errors, validity of the criticisms produced, usefulness to authors and editors, and agreement with human reviewers. Each needs its own instrument. In addition, existing evidence does not yet evaluate the specialist product's criticism validity and usefulness on real submissions: the comparisons against human reviews used the holistic arms, and the specialist pipeline has only been compared with plain review on two planted-error papers, one run each (section 2).

Main conclusions

  1. The current comparisons cannot rank configurations, though they do reveal failure mechanisms. Independent runs of the same model rarely rediscover the same real errors [1], and one run on two papers cannot separate a stage effect from run-to-run variation. Papers, not findings, judgments or runs, are the sampling unit [2, 3].
  2. Several staged reviewers beat their chosen baselines, but none isolates specialist decomposition. The strongest recall evidence is AAAI-26's five-stage pipeline, which beat a single prompt on planted-error detection in every aspect, at a much larger budget [4]. Other systems report author-rated or judge-rated gains [5, 6, 7]. On general reasoning tasks, strong single agents often match multi-agent systems at equal compute [8, 9]. This motivates testing decomposition against a budget-matched baseline but does not settle it, and nearly all the evidence comes from computer science.
  3. Discovery and finalization can both limit performance. ReviScope's specialist trace identifies discovery as the main loss of planted targets. Elsewhere, selection is the documented weak point: on ten papers, selectors that saw only the paper achieved 40–44% weighted coverage of human concerns, against 79.3% for an oracle choosing from the same candidate pool [10]. Drops from candidates to published findings mix useful filtering, deduplication and harmful loss, so each stage transition needs adjudication before effort is redirected.
  4. Precision needs adjudication of the findings themselves. Closed answer keys count every valid unmatched criticism as a false positive. The strongest precedents pool findings from all systems and humans and have experts adjudicate them blind to origin [11, 12, 13]. Adjudication must cover each system's version of a criticism, including its rationale and remedy, not just the shared issue.
  5. Utility rubrics do not measure correctness. RQI, ARCADIA, RevUtil and the 2026 editors' Delphi dimensions score coverage, specificity, actionability and manner [14, 15, 16, 17]. Fluent, specific and wrong criticism scores well on all of them.
  6. The characteristic LLM-reviewer harm is unnecessary or out-of-scope requests, the largest category (30.49%) of deficient LLM review segments in ReviewCritique [18]. COPE's distinction between work that is essential to support claims and work that would strengthen or extend it [19] gives a citable basis for scoring remedy necessity.
  7. Whole-report preference by LLM judges is confounded by length and style. Padding reviews with non-informative text raised human quality ratings from 3.73 to 4.29 [20]; 23 of 29 LLM review-quality metrics shifted under faithful rewrites [21]; AI review scores correlate more with each other (r = 0.49) than with human scores (0.15) [22]. Validity and contribution should be assessed on atomic criticisms; the delivered report still needs its own assessment for prioritisation, burden and clarity.

The practical consequence is a change of primary outcomes. Instead of asking which configuration a judge prefers, or how many planted targets were matched, ask per paper: how many adjudicated-valid, consequential criticisms and valid, beneficial suggestions does a configuration produce; how many incorrect or disproportionate ones; and at what compute? Planted-error recovery and report preference remain useful secondary and diagnostic outcomes.

3. Four outcomes that are being conflated

The literature separates four outcomes that are often treated as one. A system can do well on one and badly on another.

OutcomeWhat it asksTypical instrumentWhat it misses
Known-error recoveryDoes the review find a designated defect?Planted or real-error benchmarks; LLM or quote matching [1, 23, 24, 25]Validity of everything else the review says; materiality; non-error contributions. Depends on the key's validity.
Criticism validityIs each criticism true of the manuscript, and does its inference follow?Expert adjudication of emitted findings [11, 12, 13]Coverage (a valid but trivial review scores well); usefulness.
UsefulnessDoes it help an editor decide and an author improve?RQI, ARCADIA, RevUtil, author and editor ratings, uptake, manuscript improvement [14, 15, 16, 26, 27]Correctness. Valence bias: authors rate reviews recommending acceptance higher [20, 28].
Agreement with humansDoes it raise what human reviewers raised?Comment overlap, score correlation, decision prediction [29, 30, 31]Humans share only about 29–35% of their points with each other [29]; inter-reviewer kappa is about .17 [32]; independent committees disagreed on 23% of decisions [33]. Unmatched does not mean wrong.

Planted-error recall measures the first outcome on a key whose validity is in question. The project's AI-assisted, unblinded provisional audit sorted the 100 Dawes targets into four evidential categories: about 42 demonstrable errors (often narrower than annotated), 24 reporting gaps, 9 needing external verification and 25 weak or invalid. Those categories guide expert checking and sensitivity analyses. They are not a replacement gold standard, and the official annotation-overlap scores should be kept. AAAI-26 found a similar problem with LLM-generated perturbations: of 35 audited targets, 22 were jointly judged significant enough to warrant a review criticism, nine minor and four disputed [4]. Whole-report LLM preference mixes the third and fourth outcomes with presentation effects. Only the planned criticism-level expert adjudication measures the second.

Historical human baselines are a reference point, not a target. In BMJ studies, reviewers found on average 2.6 of 9 inserted major errors [34] and commented on about 2 of 8 planted weaknesses [35]; detection also varied with the reviewer's recommendation (39% for rejecters, 17% for accepters) [36]. These figures come from different manuscripts and error types, so they do not give an expected human performance for ReviScope's benchmarks. They do show that recall on planted errors is a narrow measure, confounded with the reviewer's overall decision.

4.2 Evidence on staged and specialist designs

Several staged reviewers outperform their chosen baselines on task-specific outcomes. The strongest recall comparison is AAAI-26's: with the same model, its pipeline detected planted errors at higher rates in all five aspects, for example 0.67 against 0.35 for story-level errors and 0.45 against 0.26 for significance [4]. Its stages run in sequence and see earlier results, which differs from independent specialist readers, and its baseline was one prompt against several high-effort stages. Narrow, targeted prompts beat general review prompts on error detection in early work [46], and generic review generators were insensitive to counterfactually inserted logic flaws [47]. Narrow per-item checks are the best-validated deployed use: over 70% of authors found a NeurIPS checklist assistant useful, with inaccuracy and over-strictness the main complaints [48].

Gains can be small when the outcome is a global score: in one study, a one-paragraph prompt nearly matched an engineered pipeline at predicting ICLR outcomes, although the pipeline's scores varied much less on reruns (median within-paper SD 0.7 against 2.8, across ten papers with two reruns each) [49]. On reasoning tasks, multi-agent discussion rarely beats a well-prompted single agent at equal compute [8, 9], and multi-agent systems fail in characteristic ways in information handoff and verification [50].

None of the review-specific comparisons surveyed here controls compute. In ReviScope's case, the specialist pilot used 42 model calls across two papers, where the plain baseline used one per paper. A specialist advantage can only be attributed to decomposition if the comparator gets a similar discovery budget (for example a broad review sampled k times, pooled and deduplicated) and the same verification and editorial treatment.

4.3 Selection and verification

Selection is a documented weak point in other systems. Besides the pre-submission pool result above [10], AAAI-26 respondents' main complaints were nitpicking, verbosity and poor prioritisation [4], and in the Gauntlet study humans won on papers where the AI produced one confident error or an unprioritised list [7]. Unfiltered tools produce false positives: 14 of 40 flagged papers in the YesNoError spot check [45]. SPOT's best precision of 6.1% is relative to its reference set, under an exhaustive-key assumption. It shows low agreement with the annotated errors, not an adjudicated false-discovery rate [1].

Almost every deployed system has a critic or verifier stage, but none reports an ablation of that stage's precision against the recall it loses. The general literature is mixed. Intrinsic self-correction can turn correct answers into wrong ones [51]. Verification with independently answered questions raised factual precision while reducing the number of facts produced [52]. Combining several imperfect verifiers, weighted by estimated accuracy, works better than any one of them [53]. LLM judges are also persuaded by rhetoric: persuasive additions inflate scores for incorrect solutions [54], and models defer to stated user views [55]. ReviScope gives the verifier the generating rationale as untrusted material, which is consistent with this evidence, but the effect of that choice has not been measured.

4.4 Tools and re-analysis

In a randomised study of 103 teams, 37% of AI-led teams (13 of 35) achieved computational reproduction, against 94% of human-only teams (31 of 33), and AI-led teams and found fewer major coding errors [56]. Coding agents given the original paper became biased toward confirming its results on tasks that could not be reproduced, and prompt framing could induce specification search [57]. The best agents on REPRO-Bench assessed reproducibility correctly in about a fifth to a third of cases [58].

4.5 Robustness

Hidden instructions can steer targeted parts of a review [59]. Presentation-only rewrites, with methods and results unchanged, raised AI review scores by 1.21 points on a ten-point scale [60]. LLM reviews echo authors' disclosed limitations far more than human reviews do [61]. An ensemble of LLM reviewers accepted about half of a set of deliberately unsound, fabricated papers while raising integrity concerns about a similar share [62]: raising a concern and weighing it in the overall judgment are separate behaviours.

5. Dimensions of review quality

No validated gold-standard instrument for review quality exists. A systematic review found 24 tools; none defined quality, and only one had documented scale development [63]. ARCADIA's definition is the most useful: a review is good to the extent that it "helps editors make a fair decision and authors improve" [15]. Quality is only measurable relative to stated objectives [64].

The framework below combines human peer-review instruments, which score whole reports, with NLP work that scores single points. It works at three levels:

  • Issue: a cluster of criticisms about the same underlying problem.
  • Variant: each system's or reviewer's version of an issue, with its own rationale, consequence and remedy.
  • Report: the whole review as delivered.

5.1 Variant-level dimensions

Clusters identify shared issues, but a supported cluster does not confer support on every member. One system's version can have a false rationale or a disproportionate remedy while another's is sound. Adjudicate each materially distinct variant, count issue recovery once per report, and attribute incorrect arguments and remedies to the outputs that contain them.

5.3 Precision and yield, defined

Keep validity and usefulness apart. Per paper, report:

  • supported / all;
  • supported / (supported + contradicted);
  • unresolved / all;
  • the number of contradicted variants;
  • supported with C2 ≥ 2 / all;
  • a separate count of serious harms (contradicted or unnecessary major or critical advice).

State how not-assessable items and empty outputs are handled. Never report resolved-only precision alone: it can improve simply by deferring difficult findings.

Statistical and methodological checking is where specialist modules are most defensible. It is under-supplied: 34% of responding leading biomedical journals (36 of 107) rarely or never use specialised statistical review [76]. In a BMJ study, training in epidemiology or statistics was the only reviewer characteristic that both editors and authors associated with higher review quality [77]. In trials, added statistical or reporting-guideline review modestly improved final manuscripts [27, 78].

Matching is itself a source of error: Liang et al.'s matcher had precision 0.78 and recall 0.88 against human labels [29]. Localisation metrics, such as FLAWS's top-k spans and CRED's deterministic quote matching, avoid an LLM matcher but ignore whether the rationale is right [12, 23]. ReviScope's quote anchoring makes localisation scoring cheap to add alongside LLM matching.

6.2 LLM judges

Documented biases include position [80], length [81], self-preference that persists when model names are hidden [82], sycophancy [55] and persuasion [54]. General-purpose judges reached only about 51–57% on hard pairs with objectively correct answers [83]. On review quality, GPT-4o agreed with trained graduate-student annotators, who saw only titles and abstracts, at τ = 0.36 for overall quality and 0.12 for factuality [84]. These results show weaknesses in the settings tested. They do not give an expected accuracy for ReviScope's judges, which need their own calibration.

Mitigations with evidence behind them:

  • decomposed checklists rather than Likert scales [85];
  • reference-guided judging [86];
  • panels of diverse judges, with individual verdicts retained [87];
  • length-controlled preference estimation as a sensitivity analysis [81];
  • invariance tests under faithful rewrites [21].

The Refine benchmark aligns atomic concerns and judges only the residual unique ones [39]. That estimates incremental supported coverage, but it does not assess the delivered report. Removing shared points can hide differences in qualification, explanation or remedy, and filtering out unsupported points hides the harm they would do. Use atomic assessment for validity and contribution. Keep a separate whole-report assessment of prioritisation, burden, uncertainty and positive feedback, in native and normalised representations as the validation plan already proposes.

Opus preferred every AI arm to both expert reports on both manuscripts. That is consistent with these biases: AI reports are longer and more structured, criticism-only presentation removes the praise and discussion in human prose, and judges share stylistic preferences with AI reviewers [22]. LLM and human critiques also have different functional profiles, and "expert" prompting amplifies LLM tendencies rather than making them more human-like [88].

6.3 Clustering criticisms

Atomic decomposition made factual precision measurable for long-form text [89]. But over-atomised claims lose the context needed to verify them, and "molecular" units that keep the necessary context were far less ambiguous [90]. For review criticisms, the unit should keep the study, passage, variable and inferential claim. Use bidirectional entailment for equivalence and directional coverage for target matching. Audit over-merging and over-splitting on a human-checked sample that includes deliberately mismatched variants. A trained alignment model is a cheap second matcher for disagreement checks [91].

6.4 Using judges without trusting them

Judges can reduce human effort if they are treated as auxiliary measurements. Prediction-powered inference combines judge labels on all items with human labels on a random subsample, and corrects the judge's bias [92]. Two-phase designs with known sampling probabilities allow oversampling where the judge is least reliable [93]. Neither resolves the dependence among variants, runs and papers, and both are fragile with few papers. Start with transparent inverse-probability-weighted totals and paper-level summaries, and treat these estimators as a later efficiency step. Whatever the estimator, the human sample must give every relevant stratum a positive, recorded inclusion probability, as the validation plan specifies: targeted strata (unique contributions of a stage, judge disagreements, suspected-incorrect major claims) plus a reproducibly sampled remainder.

7. Experimental design and statistics

7.1 Units and repetition

Papers play the role of stimuli in a participants-by-stimuli design: adding judgments, runs or criticisms for a fixed set of papers cannot raise power for generalisation beyond a ceiling set by the number of papers [2]. Compute each paper's configuration difference averaged over runs, summarise the paired differences, and show paper-level results. Normal-approximation intervals are unreliable with small evaluation sets [3], and resampling should preserve pairing [94].

Repeated generation matters for a different reason. In a study of LLM benchmarks, single runs produced a pairwise ranking inversion in ten of twelve slices relative to a three-run majority, and a second run removed most of them [95]. For review, issue-level stability matters as much as mean recall: two configurations can have the same recall while catching different issues on different runs [1].

7.2 Sample size

In a pilot, estimate the paired between-paper SD of the actual primary endpoint, annotation time and the variance components. Then simulate the intended design, with unequal sampling weights and missing ratings. As a purely illustrative calculation: if paired paper-level differences in a proportion had an SD of 0.20, a 95% interval with half-width 0.05 would need about 62 papers, from n ≈ (1.96 × sΔ / h)². Ten papers would give a half-width near 0.12. Adding planted errors to the same ten papers does not substitute for more papers.

7.3 Decision criteria

Prespecify:

  • one principal contrast between specialist and a comparator;
  • a benefit endpoint;
  • acceptable loss margins on validity precision;
  • a limit on serious incorrect advice;
  • usability requirements;
  • a cost premium.

Require the relevant confidence bounds to clear each criterion, and label other contrasts exploratory. A non-significant difference does not establish equivalence [96], and a preference win cannot compensate for materially harmful advice.

7.4 Rater reliability

Report Krippendorff's α with the appropriate distance function: nominal for support labels, ordinal for materiality [97]. Report raw agreement and prevalence alongside AC1, rather than choosing the most favourable coefficient [98]. Compute agreement on independent ratings made before discussion. For comparison, annotators of review-point utility reached α of .39–.56 [16], and refining the guidelines raised kappa for lazy-reviewing labels from .31 to .52 [72]. Budget expert time for reading the manuscripts, not only for rating packets.

8. A proposed evaluation programme

This adapts the existing validation plan, with the specialist strategy as the product under test and plain and holistic as labelled comparators.

8.2 Outcomes, in order of priority

  1. Benefit: per paper, supported issues with C2 ≥ 2, and supported, non-redundant suggestions with an expert-rated concrete benefit (C6), reported by contribution type.
  2. Harm: contradicted variants, disproportionate remedies and serious harms, attributed to the outputs that contain them. An extension is not harmful merely because it is optional or beyond scope; its validity, feasibility and proportionality still require assessment.
  3. Stage transitions:
    • Trace each issue through discovery → verifier → source gate → editorial → published or shown as unresolved.
    • Record source-access holds, required-source completion, severity holds and duplicate consolidation.
    • Adjudicate whether each transition helped or harmed.
    • Historical traces reflect the historical verifier contract, so replay frozen candidates under the current one, including true headlines with false rationales and failed source lookups.
  4. Known-error recovery by audit stratum, with localisation scoring alongside LLM matching, and official annotation-overlap scores retained. Before scoring new outputs, freeze expert-approved interpretations of each target and the narrower criticisms that count as acceptable.
  5. Stability: per-issue detection frequency across repeated runs.
  6. Reader benefit: which supported points authors or qualified readers would act on; whether they understand the action and the distinction between essential and optional work; reading time.
  7. Whole-report assessment as a diagnostic, in native and normalised representations, in both orders, with length sensitivity.
  8. Cost: calls, tokens, stage minutes, failures and expert hours.

8.3 Instruments to add

  • Counterfactual triples for social psychology. Before generation, have experts validate flawed, repaired and neutral versions of a few manuscripts (for example an overclaimed causal inference, or a construct substituted between hypothesis and measure). Measure three things, for both the relevant module and the final report [47]: correct target-specific criticism in the flawed version, inappropriate persistence after repair, and sensitivity to neutral edits. Include the original versions as negative controls.
  • Real-error items: statcheck-flagged decision errors confirmed by hand, and corrected or retracted social-psychology papers whose flaw is detectable from the manuscript [1, 79].
  • Verifier persuasion probes: hold a criticism constant and vary how persuasive the rationale is, its stated confidence and any implied expert endorsement [54, 55].
  • Presentation invariance: faithful rewrites of the same content, to measure how far judged preference moves without any change in content [21, 60].
  • Prompt injection: controlled manuscripts with hidden instructions targeting specific review fields [59].
  • Retrieval diagnostic: whether modules find and open the relevant preregistration, original study, replications, meta-analyses and cited sources. The development pilot already shows 403 errors blocking original-study checks.
  • Limitations echo: flag criticisms that restate the manuscript's own limitations. Distinguish limitations that are adequately addressed from acknowledged limitations that still undermine a claim [61].

8.4 Case sets

Name a development set, a rubric-calibration set and an untouched comparison set, and record each case's exposure history. The four reserved submitted manuscripts are two tutorials, a meta-research analysis and one two-experiment social psychology paper. Report tutorials, methodological papers and empirical submissions separately, and do not treat their pooled variance as representative of ordinary empirical submissions. Define the confirmatory sample by field and design, following the sampling frame, rather than by what archives happen to offer.

8.5 Sequence

  1. Adjudicate first. Have experts adjudicate 50–100 issue packets from existing development outputs, including specialist traces, and audit their stage transitions. Draw them from the validation plan's targeted strata plus a reproducibly sampled remainder, with inclusion probabilities recorded, and keep rubric-calibration packets separate from the packets used to estimate performance. Calibrate the current complete-criticism verifier on the probe set. This needs no new review runs.
  2. Small practical comparison. Run frozen specialist, plain and holistic on a few untouched cases, with at least two runs each, and estimate endpoint variance and annotation time. Budget model calls and expert hours in advance.
  3. Architectural controls where needed. Add budget-matched broad sampling only if the practical comparison leaves the decomposition question open. Define the budget in advance (model tokens including reasoning, or monetary cost; latency separately) and report actual consumption per arm.
  4. Preregister and expand. Expand only once benefit, harm and cost meet the prespecified advancement criteria, then run the confirmatory series on unseen cases across the profile family.

9. Hypotheses for the specialist pipeline

These are suggested by the literature. Test them in the programme above rather than adopting them as defaults.

  • Frame module rubrics as checkable questions. Targeted error-finding prompts outperform general review prompts [4, 46], and question-tree decomposition beat multi-agent discussion at lower cost [6]. Keep room for broad conceptual synthesis, and test whether checklists lose conceptual criticism. An "assessed, no issue" ledger entry does not establish that the check succeeded.
  • Locate losses before redistributing effort. The specialist trace points to discovery; other systems point to selection [7, 10]. Adjudicating stage transitions (8.2, item 3) decides which applies here.
  • Treat deterministic outputs as reproducible leads. Confirm extraction, test identity, rounding, sidedness and any applicable corrections before establishing an inconsistency; then assess its consequence. statcheck's validation authors recommend checking individual flags by hand [41, 44].
  • Separate recomputation from the claim. Run recomputation in a separate context, with frozen inputs and an explicit analysis specification, before comparing with the reported value [57]. Hiding the reported number alone does not prevent specification search.
  • Measure verifier behaviour per model family on the probe set before choosing a cross-family verifier. Families differ markedly in how lenient they are when scoring manuscripts [99, 100], though score leniency is not the same as falsely accepting criticisms.
  • Label remedy necessity in the output as essential, strengthening or extending, following COPE [19]. This addresses the characteristic LLM failure [18] and makes C5 directly scoreable.
  • Use figures and tables. Text-only anchoring misses evidence in tables and figures [101, 102].

10. Open questions

  • How complete must the reference set be? Pooled adjudication improves coverage but favours systems that contributed to the pool. A leave-one-system-out sensitivity analysis shows by how much [11].
  • Where is the line between an error, a limitation and an unreasonable demand? Expert disagreement may reflect legitimate judgments about scope rather than annotation noise [47, 103].
  • Can consensus establish validity? Judge panels and checklists raise consistency more readily than correctness, and model families may share blind spots [85, 87].
  • Does precision transfer across prevalence? Precision on flawed or AI-generated papers may differ sharply from precision on mostly sound submissions [12].
  • Do human-review corpora remain usable? Training reviewers on AI-generated reviews compresses ratings and reduces diversity [75], and public review corpora increasingly contain LLM-modified text.
  • Does evidence transfer from ML papers to social psychology? Almost all comparative design evidence comes from computer science. The strongest social-science evidence concerns reproducibility, not review [12, 56, 58].
